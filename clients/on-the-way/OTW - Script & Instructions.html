On The Way AC Plumbing & Electric

Video Scripts, Version 2. Production.
14 scripts across 7 services. Shot direction, lines and production notes.
Shot by a company videographer on location. Not self-recorded. 45 second hard cap, and all 14 land between 34 and 42 seconds. Every script closes on the same CTA: "Tap Learn More and talk to us right now." Meta, so no phone number on any end card. Two shapes per service while the owner-on-camera question is open: one owner on camera, one voiceover over b-roll.

AC / HVAC

Script 1: "Thursday"

Angle: The wait, not the breakdown. Highest-intent moment there is.
On camera: The owner, full-body walk-n-talk.
Runtime: Approx. 42 seconds. 105 words.
HOOK (0 to 3 seconds)
Owner walks out of the shop bay in Santa Fe, straight at camera, steadicam pulling back ahead of him. Branded truck visible over his shoulder.
"It's a hundred degrees out, your AC quit sometime last night, and the company you called can't get here till Thursday."
BEAT 2
He keeps walking, reaches the truck, puts a hand on the door. Camera settles to a medium.
"We're out of Santa Fe and we cover the whole county. League City, Friendswood, Texas City, Galveston."
BEAT 3
B-roll: truck rolling down a residential street, morning light. Quick cut to a tech stepping down from the cab with a bag.
"Get ahold of us during the day, you're usually talking to somebody within the hour. Most days we're at your house that same afternoon."
BEAT 4
B-roll continues: night exterior, porch light on, truck pulling up in the dark. Tech at the condenser unit with a work light.
"And if it's two in the morning and the house is already at ninety, somebody answers then too. That's what the twenty-four seven actually means."
BEAT 5
Back to the owner, leaning on the truck, closer framing. Direct to camera.
"Your AC didn't wait for business hours. We don't either. Tap Learn More and talk to us right now."

On-screen text: Santa Fe, TX / Same day, most days / 24/7 emergency

End card: Arrow pointing down-left to the Learn More button. No phone number.

Production notes: The night footage is what sells this, so it's worth the second setup rather than faking dusk. The two in the morning line has to land over a real dark exterior or it reads as a claim instead of a fact. Owner should say it walking, not standing still.

Script 2: "Pointing fingers"

Angle: The three licenses, aimed at AC specifically. The one no competitor can run.
On camera: Nobody until the CTA. Owner voiceover over b-roll.
Runtime: Approx. 37 seconds. 89 words.
HOOK (0 to 3 seconds)
Tight on a breaker panel. A hand flips the breaker back. Hold one beat. It trips again. Cut to black for a frame.
"Your AC keeps tripping the breaker."
BEAT 2
B-roll, played almost like a re-enactment: a truck with no branding pulls away from a house. Homeowner standing in the doorway with her arms crossed.
"So the AC company comes out, pokes around, tells you it's electrical, call an electrician. Electrician comes out, tells you it's the AC."
BEAT 3
Interior, hot. Ceiling fan going. Someone asleep on a downstairs couch, blanket half off.
"Meanwhile you're a week in, sleeping downstairs where it's cooler."
BEAT 4
Hard tonal shift. On The Way truck pulls in, door opens, uniform, tool bag. Cut, cut, cut: tech at the condenser, tech at the panel, tech at the thermostat. Fast, competent, no talking.
"We've got all three state licenses. AC, plumbing, electric. Same company, same truck."
BEAT 5
Slow push on the two techs working the unit and the panel at the same time, both in frame.
"So when it's not clear whose problem it is, that's fine. It's all our problem. We just find it and fix it."
BEAT 6
First and only face. Owner, on camera, at the truck.
"One company, all three trades. Tap Learn More and talk to us right now."

On-screen text: AC / PLUMBING / ELECTRIC (stamped one at a time on the license line)

End card: Arrow pointing down-left to the Learn More button.

Production notes: The whole thing rests on Beat 4's tonal turn, so Beats 2 and 3 need to feel genuinely annoying to sit through. Two techs in one frame in Beat 5 is the proof shot and it can't be faked with a cutaway. If the licenses are physically on the truck or on cards, shoot them.

Plumbing

Script 3: "Standing in the shower"

Angle: Water heater, same day. The volume play, highest-frequency plumbing job there is.
On camera: The owner, full-body walk-n-talk.
Runtime: Approx. 39 seconds. 93 words.
HOOK (0 to 3 seconds)
Cold open on a shower head, water running. Steam nowhere. A hand goes into the stream and yanks straight back out. Cut to owner mid-stride in a driveway, camera walking backward ahead of him.
"You found out your water heater died the way everybody finds out. Standing in the shower."
BEAT 2
He's walking toward the house, tool bag over his shoulder. Homeowner holds the door.
"Most of the time we can fix that today. Tank, tankless, doesn't matter, we work on both."
BEAT 3
B-roll: tech in the garage with the panel off the unit, flashlight, hands working. Then a beat of him turning to the homeowner and pointing at something, talking, her nodding.
"We'll tell you what it is and what it costs before we touch anything. You say go or you don't."
BEAT 4
New tank coming off the truck on a dolly, up the driveway. Quick, competent.
"If it's shot, we'll put a new one in, and there's financing if you need it."
BEAT 5
Owner back on camera, at the truck. Close. A kid runs past behind him in the background if you can get it naturally.
"Nobody should be heating water on the stove to give their kid a bath. Tap Learn More and talk to us right now."

On-screen text: Tank & tankless / Same day, most days / Licensed RMP43535

End card: Arrow pointing down-left to the Learn More button.

Production notes: The cold-open shower shot is the whole hook and it's three seconds, so light it properly rather than grabbing it. Beat 3 needs the tech actually explaining something to the homeowner before work starts, because that shot IS the upfront-pricing claim. Don't stage it as a handshake. The kid in Beat 5 is a bonus, not a requirement, and a fake one is worse than none.

Script 4: "The warm spot"

Angle: Slab leaks and the fear of demolition. Precision, high ticket, and nobody generic can run it.
On camera: Nobody until the CTA. Owner voiceover over b-roll.
Runtime: Approx. 42 seconds. 101 words.
HOOK (0 to 3 seconds)
Close on a water bill on a kitchen counter. A finger stops on the number. Hold.
"Your water bill doubled for no reason."
BEAT 2
Bare foot steps onto tile in a hallway. Stops. Shifts back. Holds there.
"There's a warm spot on the floor by the hallway."
BEAT 3
Quiet house at night. Nothing running. A woman standing still in a dark kitchen, listening. Sound design carries this one.
"And you can hear water running when nothing's on."
BEAT 4
Cut to a jackhammer sitting on a living room floor next to a pile of broken tile. Ugly. Hold it one beat too long, then cut away hard.
"That's a slab leak. And the first thing everybody pictures is a guy with a jackhammer taking apart the living room floor to go find it."
BEAT 5
Tonal shift. Tech kneeling with headphones on, ground microphone against the slab, eyes closed, listening. Slow. Then he opens his eyes and marks a small X on the tile with a pencil.
"We find it first. We've got equipment that listens through the slab, so we know exactly where it is before anybody breaks anything."
BEAT 6
The finished work: one small, neat opening in the floor. Tech's hands on the repair. Then wide of the room, mostly intact.
"Then we open the smallest hole that gets the job done."
BEAT 7
Owner, on camera, at the truck. First and only face.
"Your floor is not a search area. Tap Learn More and talk to us right now."

On-screen text: Electronic leak detection / We find it before we open it / Licensed RMP43535

End card: Arrow pointing down-left to the Learn More button.

Production notes: Beat 5 is the entire ad. The pencil X on the tile is the money shot, because it's precision made visible in one frame, and it's what Beat 4's jackhammer was set up to contrast against. Beat 3 lives or dies on sound design, so record real room tone. The jackhammer in Beat 4 should look like a threat, not like work.

VERIFY BEFORE SHOOTING: Written as listening through the slab with a ground mic, which is how acoustic detection normally works, but their site only says "electronic leak detection" without naming the method. If their techs use something that looks different on camera, this load-bearing beat falls apart on the day.

Angle: The electrical problems people just live with. Volume play, instant recognition.
On camera: The owner, full-body walk-n-talk.

Owner mid-stride up a driveway, camera walking backward ahead of him.
"Same with the breaker you reset every time somebody runs the microwave and the toaster together. You worked around it."
BEAT 3
B-roll: garage at night, someone in a robe flipping a breaker back with a phone flashlight in their teeth.
"That's all the same job, and it's usually a couple hours of work."
BEAT 4
Tech with a multimeter, actually testing. Then pulling an outlet out of the wall and turning it over to look at the back. Real diagnosis, not a montage.
"We'll actually find what's causing it, instead of just swapping the outlet and hoping."
BEAT 5
Owner on camera at the truck. Close.
"You've been living around your own house long enough. Tap Learn More and talk to us right now."

On-screen text: Dead outlets / Flickering lights / Breakers that won't stay on

End card: Arrow pointing down-left to the Learn More button.

Production notes: The stretched cord in the hook is the whole ad in one frame, so shoot it wide enough that you can see how far it's reaching. Beat 3's garage-at-night shot should feel undignified, because that's the point. In Beat 4 the multimeter has to be doing something real, since an actor waving a tool at a wall reads as fake to exactly the audience that has this problem.

Script 6: "The boring way"

Angle: Permits. Almost nobody advertises the unglamorous version of this.
On camera: Nobody until the CTA. Owner voiceover over b-roll.

BEAT 3
A kitchen table. Paperwork spread out. Two people sitting across from someone with a folder. Neutral, quiet, tense.
"Or you file a claim and the adjuster asks who pulled the permit."
BEAT 4
Tonal shift. On The Way tech at a clean panel. Every breaker seated straight, printed labels going on one at a time. Slow, methodical, almost satisfying.
"We do it the boring way. Two hundred amp panel, permit filed, inspection scheduled, and it's on record."
BEAT 5
A permit card going up on the panel door. A hand smoothing it flat. Then an inspector signing off and handing the sheet over.
"It costs more than a Saturday. It costs a lot less than finding out later."
BEAT 6
Owner, on camera, at the truck. First and only face.
"Tap Learn More and talk to us right now."

On-screen text: 200A upgrades / Permit filed / Inspection scheduled

End card: Arrow pointing down-left to the Learn More button.

Production notes: The Sharpie label with the question mark is worth hunting for, because it does more work than any line in the script. Beat 4 should be shot like a watch being assembled, slow and precise, since the whole contrast is competence against improvisation. Don't let the inspector in Beat 2 mug for the camera. The flatter he plays it, the worse it feels.

CLIENT CHECK, TWO ITEMS: The adjuster line in Beat 3 gestures at insurance trouble from unpermitted work. Written as a question the adjuster asks, not a claim you'd be denied, which keeps it defensible. Still worth the client's eyes. Cuts cleanly. Second, "It costs more than a Saturday" in Beat 5 openly admits they're not the cheap option, which is what buys credibility for the rest. Some owners hate seeing that in their own ad.

Roofing

Script 7: "Since 1976"

Angle: The storm chaser contrast. Only a company that's actually been here can run this.
On camera: The owner, full-body walk-n-talk.
Runtime: Approx. 40 seconds. 96 words.
HOOK (0 to 3 seconds)
Close on a license plate from another state, on a pickup rolling slow down a residential street. Pull up to a guy with a clipboard already walking up somebody's driveway. Shot from across the street, slightly hidden, like you're watching him.
"After the next big storm, there's going to be somebody in your driveway with plates from three states away."
BEAT 2
A single shingle lifting and flapping in wind. Then a wide of an empty driveway, nobody in it, tire marks and a scatter of trash.
"He'll do your roof cash, and he'll be gone before the first shingle lifts."
BEAT 3
Hard tonal shift. Owner on camera, walking the lot at the Santa Fe shop. The building, the sign, the address. If there's anything old in that building, it's in this shot.
"We've been in Santa Fe since 1976. Fifty years, same town, same address."
BEAT 4
Crew on a roof. Bundles going up the ladder with the Owens Corning and GAF wrapping visible. Nail guns, rhythm, real work.
"We put on Owens Corning and GAF. Those are rated for a hundred and thirty mile an hour wind, because we know what comes through here."
BEAT 5
Owner at the truck, shop and street sign behind him. Wide enough that you can tell it's a real place.
"When your roof needs looking at again in ten years, you'll know where to find us. Tap Learn More and talk to us right now."

On-screen text: Santa Fe, TX since 1976 / Owens Corning & GAF / 130 MPH rated

End card: Arrow pointing down-left to the Learn More button.

Production notes: If anything from the early years still exists, it is the most valuable thing on this shoot. An original sign, a founder's photo on the office wall, an old truck, a framed first license, a grandfather who still comes by. Ask before the day, not on it. Fifty years is a claim until you put it on screen, and then it's a fact. Beat 1's watching-from-across-the-street framing does a lot of work, so don't shoot it clean and friendly.

STRONGER VARIANT, PENDING VERIFICATION: If the client confirms the business was doing this work through the big ones, swap Beat 3 for: "We've been in Santa Fe since 1976. We were here for Alicia, we were here for Ike, we were here for Harvey." Named storms land far harder in Galveston County than a number does. Their generator page already references Harvey by name, so they're comfortable invoking it. Do not shoot unverified.

Script 8: "Either way you stop wondering"

Angle: The free inspection, aimed at the dread of the insurance process rather than the damage.
On camera: Nobody until the CTA. Owner voiceover over b-roll.
Runtime: Approx. 35 seconds. 84 words.
HOOK (0 to 3 seconds)
Homeowner in the front yard, hand up shading her eyes, looking at the roof. Hold. Cut to a gutter full of shingle granules, and granules scattered on the driveway like sand.
"You're pretty sure the roof took a hit in the last storm."
BEAT 2
Someone sitting on the bottom stair with a phone on speaker beside them. Hold music. They're just waiting. Nothing happens.
"You're also pretty sure you don't want to deal with your insurance company about it."
BEAT 3
Ladder going up against the eave. Boots on the ladder. Tech onto the roof.
"So we'll get up there and look. Doesn't cost you anything."
BEAT 4
Tech on the roof photographing a hail strike with his phone. Chalk circle around it. A tape measure across a section. Notes on a tablet. Methodical and unhurried.
"If there's damage, we get pictures of all of it. Measurements, the whole file, and we're there when your adjuster comes out."
BEAT 5
The money shot. Tech comes back down the ladder, walks over to the homeowner, and shakes his head. Small shrug. Then he just walks to the truck and leaves.
"If there's nothing, we tell you there's nothing and we leave."
BEAT 6
Owner, on camera, at the truck. First and only face.
"Either way you stop wondering. Tap Learn More and talk to us right now."

On-screen text: Free roof inspection / Full storm documentation / Local since 1976

End card: Arrow pointing down-left to the Learn More button.

Production notes: Beat 5 is the entire ad and it's a roofer walking away from a job, which is the single most trust-building image available in that trade. Play it completely flat. No warm music swell, no handshake, no reassuring nod. He shrugs and he goes. Beat 2 needs real hold music and real silence, because the funny part is that nothing happens.

LEGAL NOTE: The adjuster wording is deliberately hedged. Texas restricts roofing contractors from acting as public adjusters on claims they're bidding, so "we handle your claim" language is a real risk. Their own site says "assistance and coordination" and "streamline," which reads like someone already knew. This matches that hedge.

Insulation

Script 9: "Maybe"

Angle: The anti-upsell. They sell AC and insulation, so they're the only ones who can tell you not to buy the bigger unit and have it mean something.
On camera: The owner, walk-n-talk. Only works from a face.
Runtime: Approx. 40 seconds. 96 words.
HOOK (0 to 3 seconds)
Close on a thermostat. Set to 68. Reading 78. Hold long enough to sting. Cut to the condenser outside running flat out in white sun.
"Your AC runs all day and the house never really gets cool, so somebody told you it's time for a bigger unit."
BEAT 2
Owner on camera in a driveway, a new condenser sitting on the truck bed behind him. He glances back at it. Small shrug. Completely level delivery.
"Maybe. We sell those too."
BEAT 3
Hard cut to the attic. Tech's face, headlamp, already sweating. Then a thermometer hanging from a rafter. Push in on the number until it fills frame.
"But your attic is a hundred and forty degrees up there right now. If there's four inches of insulation between that and your ceiling, no AC is going to win that fight."
BEAT 4
Attic floor. Sparse, matted insulation with the joists standing clear above it. Tech's hand pressing down on it and it barely moves.
"So we look up there first. That one's on us."
BEAT 5
Owner at the truck, close.
"You want R-38 and up down here. Go stick your head in the attic and look at yours. Tap Learn More and talk to us right now."

On-screen text: Free attic assessment / R-38 to R-60 for the Gulf Coast / Owens Corning AttiCat Certified

End card: Arrow pointing down-left to the Learn More button.

Production notes: "Maybe. We sell those too." is the whole script and it's four words, so it has to be thrown away, not performed. If he sells it, the ad dies. Don't claim the attic temperature, film it. Hang a real thermometer, shoot whatever it actually reads that day, and cut the line to match the number. A claim you can read off the wall stops being a claim.

Script 10: "If you can see the wood"

Angle: The presence of insulation isn't the same as enough insulation. Gives the viewer a test they'll fail.
On camera: Nobody until the CTA. Owner voiceover over b-roll.
Runtime: Approx. 35 seconds. 85 words.
HOOK (0 to 3 seconds)
POV. An attic hatch pushes open, head and flashlight come up through it. The beam sweeps across a lumpy grey attic. It looks fine. That's the joke.
"You stuck your head in the attic once, saw insulation up there, and figured you were covered."
BEAT 2
Slow pan across the attic floor at joist level. The tops of the joists run clean above the insulation the whole way. Let the viewer notice it before the line lands.
"Easy test. If you can see the wood, you don't have enough."
BEAT 3
Close details: two strips of old batt that don't meet, a fist-sized gap of bare ceiling between them. A hand pressing on the batt, flattening it to nothing.
"That stuff went in as strips before you owned the house. It settles, it pulls apart, it flattens out."
BEAT 4
The satisfying part. AttiCat machine running in the driveway, hose feeding up through the hatch. Then in the attic: pink fiberglass pouring out of the hose, filling in around everything, even and deep.
"We blow in Owens Corning AttiCat. It goes in everywhere, no gaps, buries the whole floor, R-38 and better."
BEAT 5
The proof. Same pan as Beat 2, same attic, joists now completely buried. Match the framing exactly.
"Most houses, one day, and we're gone."
BEAT 6
Owner, on camera, at the truck. First and only face.
"Tap Learn More and talk to us right now."

On-screen text: Owens Corning AttiCat / R-38 to R-60 / Most homes, one day

End card: Arrow pointing down-left to the Learn More button.

Production notes: Beat 5 has to be the identical shot as Beat 2, same lens, same position, same light, so the only thing that changed is the insulation. That match cut is the proof and a different angle throws it away. Shoot Beat 2 before the crew touches anything or you don't get the ad. The pink fill in Beat 4 is genuinely satisfying footage, so give it more seconds than it needs.

Rain coming down hard. A back door opens, a guy in a rain jacket goes out with a flashlight in his mouth and yanks the pull cord on a portable generator. Nothing. He yanks it again.

"and you're back out there every eight hours with a gas can, hoping the station down the road has power."
BEAT 4
Hard shift. Daylight, calm. A Generac sitting on its pad beside the house, clean and quiet. Then interior: a kitchen, the lights blink off and instantly back on. Nobody at the table looks up. Hold on them not reacting.
"A Generac sits outside on a pad, runs off the gas line you already have, and starts by itself in ten seconds. You don't do anything. You might sleep right through it."
BEAT 5
An ordinary sunny Tuesday. The unit hums to life by itself for a few seconds. Empty yard, nobody watching it. Then it shuts off.
"It tests itself every week so it works the night it matters."
BEAT 6
Owner, on camera, at the truck. First and only face.
"Tap Learn More and talk to us right now."

On-screen text: Generac Authorized Dealer / Starts in 10 seconds / Runs on your gas line, no refueling

End card: Arrow pointing down-left to the Learn More button.

Production notes: Beat 4's kitchen is the entire ad. The lights blink, they come back, and nobody looks up. That non-reaction is the product. Don't cut to a relieved smile, don't let anyone say anything, just hold on people continuing to eat. Beats 1 through 3 have to earn it by being properly unpleasant, so shoot them wet and shoot them cold. Beat 5 is worth the setup because a machine testing itself in an empty yard is quietly reassuring in a way no line could be.

KILLED LINE, DO NOT REINSTATE: Beat 4 originally read "you might not even wake up." Fine sentence anywhere except a generator ad, where portable units genuinely kill people with carbon monoxide every hurricane season.

Energy Audits

Script 13: "Nothing sounds wrong"

Angle: Ductwork. Your AC is fine and a chunk of what it makes never reaches you. Distinct from insulation: delivery, not the barrier.
On camera: Nobody until the CTA. Owner voiceover over b-roll.
Runtime: Approx. 41 seconds. 99 words.
HOOK (0 to 3 seconds)
Condenser outside, running clean. Cut inside to a hand held up to a supply vent. The air is moving. It's cold. Everything looks correct. Hold on the hand.
"Your AC is working. It's making cold air right now."
BEAT 2
Attic. Camera moves through the dark and finds a flex duct that's come off its collar, hanging loose, just blowing into the insulation. Tech puts his hand in front of the open end and you watch him react to cold air in the hottest place in the house.
"Some of that cold air is going into your attic through a duct that came apart up there, and you're paying for every bit of it."
BEAT 3
Electric meter, dial turning. Then a bill face down on a counter that nobody's picked up.
"Ducts can lose twenty to thirty percent of what your AC makes."
BEAT 4
Back to that first room. Same vent, same cold air, someone walking past it, totally unbothered. Nothing to see.
"You'd never know. Nothing sounds wrong, nothing looks wrong, the bill's just high."
BEAT 5
Tech at a kitchen table sliding a printed report across to the homeowner. Close on the page: a numbered list, each line with a cost next to it. Her finger moving down it.
"That's what an audit finds. We check the ducts, the leaks, all of it. Then you get a written list of what's costing you and what it costs to fix."
BEAT 6
Owner, on camera, at the truck. First and only face.
"Tap Learn More and talk to us right now."

On-screen text: Ducts can lose 20 to 30% / Written report, ranked / Local since 1976

End card: Arrow pointing down-left to the Learn More button.

Production notes: The tech's hand in front of the loose duct in Beat 2 is the whole ad, and it works precisely because a disconnected duct is visually obvious without any equipment. Don't reach for a thermal camera unless they actually own one, since a tool they don't have on a job they didn't do is the fastest way to make a true ad feel fake. Beat 4 has to be boring on purpose. The point is that a working system and a broken one look identical from the couch.
